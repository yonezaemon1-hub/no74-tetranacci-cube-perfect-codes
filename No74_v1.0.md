# Exact 1-perfect-code classification of the fourth-order generalized Fibonacci cubes

Ryutaro Yonezu — Independent Researcher

Manuscript: 2026-11-18 — version 1.0 — AI-assisted preprint — NOT PEER REVIEWED

## Abstract
We give an elementary, computer-independent proof that the induced subgraph of the binary n-cube on words avoiding 1111 has a 1-perfect code exactly when n=1 or n=3, for positive n. The proof combines a low-weight counting obstruction with a parity identity on the ambient cube and two local boundary obstructions. The included scripts redundantly verify the finite cases and local patterns. Independent AI audit reported PASS for the mathematical proof; author-reported Windows verification passed. Novelty remains INCONCLUSIVE; references are PARTIAL. Human peer review and proof-assistant formal verification have not been performed. No priority claim.

## Model
Let G_n=Q_n(1111), with vertex set V_n consisting of all length-n binary words having no substring 1111. Edges join words at Hamming distance one. A 1-perfect code C is a set whose closed neighborhoods partition V_n. Identify a word with its set of 1-coordinates. The allowed sets form a down-set. For x,y in V_n, deleting x\\y and then adding y\\x gives an allowed path of length |x symmetric_difference y|, through x intersection y. Thus graph and Hamming distances coincide, and distinct codewords have Hamming distance at least 3.

## Lemma 1 (low-weight necessary condition)
If C exists, n is congruent to 1 or 3 modulo 6.

The empty set is covered either by itself or by a singleton.

Case A: the empty set is a codeword. No singleton or pair is a codeword by the distance condition. Every pair must then be covered by a triple containing it. Therefore the triples that are codewords partition the edges of K_n into triangles. At every vertex, n-1 is even; and n(n-1)/2 is divisible by 3. These two conditions imply n=1 or 3 modulo 6 (including n=1, where the empty edge partition is allowed).

Case B: {i} is the unique codeword covering the empty set. Every singleton {j}, j != i, must be covered by a pair containing j and not i: no other singleton or the empty set can be a codeword, and a pair containing i would be adjacent to {i}. The pairs that are codewords therefore form a perfect matching M on the n-1 coordinates other than i. In particular n is odd. A pair {j,k} avoiding i and not in M is not itself a codeword and neither of its singleton subsets is a codeword. Its covering codeword must be a triple containing j,k. This triple cannot contain i (distance two from {i}), and cannot contain a matching edge (distance one from that codeword). Consequently these triples partition the edges of K_(n-1) minus M. Their number of edges, (n-1)(n-3)/2, is divisible by 3. This, together with odd n, implies n=1 or 3 modulo 6. When n=1 the matching and residual edge set are empty, with the same conclusion. All singleton and pair vertices used above are allowed; in fact the argument applies to any down-set containing all pairs.

## Lemma 2 (boundary parity)
Suppose n is odd. A forbidden vertex f that is the unique forbidden Hamming neighbor of some allowed vertex w must have exactly one codeword at Hamming distance one.

Over F_2 let A be the adjacency matrix of the entire n-cube, H=I+A, and extend the indicator c of C to be zero on forbidden vertices. Define e=1+Hc. The coordinate flips are commuting involutions, so A^2=nI in characteristic two and H^2=(n+1)I=0. Also H1=(n+1)1=0. Hence He=0. For each allowed vertex x, exact covering gives e(x)=0. Evaluating He at w leaves only e(f), so e(f)=0. Thus Hc(f)=1 over F_2. Since c(f)=0, an odd number of codewords are neighbors of f. Two such neighbors would be at Hamming distance at most two, contradicting the code distance bound. The number is exactly one.

## Lemma 3 (dimension seven)
For n=7 take the four forbidden words f=11111ab, with a,b in {0,1}. For each, w=11101ab is allowed and has f as its unique forbidden neighbor. Indeed, w has a zero at position 4 separating two blocks of length three, and flipping any other bit cannot produce four consecutive ones. Lemma 2 forces a codeword at distance one from each f. To eliminate both runs on positions 1..4 and 2..5, the flip must be in their intersection {2,3,4}. Four forbidden words require four choices among three positions. A repeated flip position gives two distinct codewords whose Hamming distance equals that of the corresponding forbidden words, at most two, impossible.

## Lemma 4 (odd dimensions at least nine)
For n>=8 append n-8 zeros to all words in the following table (the application of Lemma 2 is only for odd n).

| forbidden f | allowed w |
|---|---|
|11110000|11010000|
|11110001|11010001|
|11110011|11010011|
|11110101|11010101|
|11111001|11011001|

In each row w is obtained by flipping position 3. Direct inspection of its eight single-bit flips shows that the only forbidden neighbor in eight dimensions is f: only the zero at position 3 can complete a run of four ones. Appending zeros preserves this property: a flip after position 8 cannot create four consecutive ones, as the terminal run in each eight-bit witness has length at most two and newly appended positions were all zero. Flips among the first eight cannot create a run ending beyond position 8 while the appended zeros are unchanged. Thus Lemma 2 applies. The five forbidden words agree in their first four bits, and their suffixes have pairwise distance at most two (the suffix 0001 is at distance at most one from each other suffix). Each adjacent allowed codeword must flip one of the first four positions to eliminate their shared forbidden block. Five choices among four positions give a repeated position and therefore two distinct codewords at distance at most two, impossible.

## Theorem
For every n>=1, Q_n(1111) admits a 1-perfect code if and only if n is 1 or 3.

For n=1, {0} is a perfect code; for n=3, {000,111} is a perfect code since all words are allowed. Lemma 1 excludes every even n and n=5. Lemma 3 excludes n=7, and Lemma 4 excludes every remaining odd n>=9. The proof is independent of any finite computation.

## Relation to prior work (partial)
Ashrafi, Azarija, Babai, Fathalikhani and Klavzar, The (non-)existence of perfect codes in Fibonacci cubes, Information Processing Letters 116 (2016), 387–390, DOI 10.1016/j.ipl.2016.01.010, classify the ordinary forbidden-11 case and suggest investigating local obstructions in generalized Fibonacci cubes.

Michel Mollard, The existence of perfect codes in a family of generalized Fibonacci cubes, Information Processing Letters (2018), DOI 10.1016/j.ipl.2018.07.010, arXiv:1801.04106, Corollary 2.3, proves existence for n=2^p-1 and s>=3*2^(p-2), p>=2. For s=4 the sufficient condition only applies at p=2 (n=3). Section 3 also gives a code for n=7 and s=5. Neither existence statement contradicts the present theorem. Conjecture 3.1 in the arXiv version asserts that a perfect code in the forbidden-1^s graph for n>=3 must have n=2^p-1 and must also be a perfect code in the full cube. The present classification establishes the fixed-s=4 instance: its only admissible n>=3 is n=3, where the forbidden word is longer than the ambient words, and the two graphs coincide. This does not establish the general conjecture. Among the conventional parameters s>=2, the n=7 existence example of Mollard, the ordinary Fibonacci classification, and the author's earlier s=3 classification together with the present s=4 theorem give minimum s=5 for dimension seven.

The author's earlier No.71, Exact 1-Perfect-Code Classification of the Tribonacci Cubes Q_n(111), DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24295940, concerns s=3. Its proof shares low-weight triangle and matching constraints, but its further argument forces a singleton at an endpoint and uses an odd fan of four-element vertices. The present proof instead uses an ambient-cube F_2 boundary identity and two local pigeonhole obstructions. The s=3 proof is not used in the main s=4 theorem. The No.71 v1.0 manuscript was inspected for this comparison.

A targeted search for direct s=4 classifications and subsequent generalized Fibonacci-cube perfect-code results located no direct classification, but the search was not exhaustive. Related work includes Mollard, The (non-)existence of perfect codes in Lucas cubes, arXiv:2004.10198 (cyclic rather than path boundary). No priority claim is made; NOVELTY INCONCLUSIVE, REFERENCES PARTIAL. The general case s>=5 is not classified here.
